Introduction

On this page I build two desktop computers with Intel Core Ultra (Series 2) processors. The first one is for an office user (office apps, email, web browser and video calls). The second one is for me, as a developer who trains AI models. I explain why I chose each part, and you can see the budget at the end of each computer.

Important: RAM is very expensive in 2026

Since autumn 2025, the price of DDR5 memory has gone up a lot, because manufacturers give priority to memory for AI servers. TrendForce expects prices to rise by up to 45% by the end of 2026 [3]. Because of this, a 16 GB kit that used to cost about €60 now costs around €300 [6]. This makes both budgets higher than normal.

Processor, socket and chipset

Before choosing the motherboard, you need to know which socket the processor uses. According to Intel, Core Ultra (Series 2) processors use the LGA1851 socket, and this socket only works with 800 series chipsets [1][2].

Why H810 for the office?

  • Reason 1: the Core Ultra 5 225 is not a K model, so you cannot overclock it. Paying for B860 or Z890 would be a waste of money.
  • Reason 2: H810 has 8 PCIe 4.0 lanes in the chipset [2]. That is enough for one SSD, USB and network. An office PC does not need more.

Why Z890 for AI?

  • Reason 1: it is the only consumer chipset that allows CPU overclocking, which is perfect for the 265K [1].
  • Reason 2: it has 24 PCIe 4.0 lanes, compared to 14 on B860 [2]. This means you can add more NVMe SSDs to store datasets and models.

PC 1 · Office

User profile

  • Use: Word, Excel, email, a web browser with many tabs, and video calls
  • Priority: reliable, quiet, cheap and low power use
  • Doesn't need: a dedicated graphics card or overclocking

Why I chose each part

Processor · Intel Core Ultra 5 225

It has 10 cores (6 performance cores and 4 efficiency cores) and reaches 4.9 GHz [7]. This is more than enough for office work. The most important thing is that it has integrated graphics, so you don't need to buy a graphics card. Also, the Box version comes with a cooler, so you save on another part.

Motherboard · MSI PRO H810M-B

It uses the LGA1851 socket and the H810 chipset (see the explanation above). It is Micro ATX, supports DDR5, has 2.5 Gb networking and an M.2 slot for the SSD. It is one of the cheapest LGA1851 motherboards on the market.

RAM · Kingston FURY Beast DDR5 16 GB (2×8 GB) 5600 MHz

Today, 16 GB is the minimum to use Teams or Zoom together with the browser without the PC slowing down. I chose two modules so the memory works in dual channel, which makes the integrated graphics faster. I didn't choose 32 GB because, with current prices, the RAM alone would cost almost €600.

Storage · Kingston NV3 500 GB NVMe

An NVMe SSD lets Windows start in a few seconds. 500 GB is enough because, in an office, files are usually saved in the cloud or on the company server.

Power supply · Tempest PSU PRO 650W 80+ Bronze

The processor uses about 65 W in normal use, so 650 W gives a lot of extra room and the power supply stays cool and quiet. It has 80 Plus Bronze certification and more than 1,800 reviews with a rating of 4.6 out of 5 [12].

Case · Tempest Compact Shade RGB

It is compact, supports Micro ATX motherboards and is cheap. The case doesn't need a lot of airflow, because the computer has no graphics card and produces very little heat.

PC 2 · AI Developer

User profile

  • Use: training and fine-tuning AI models with PyTorch, programming and using Docker
  • Priority: a GPU with a lot of VRAM, a fast SSD and good cooling for training sessions that last for hours
  • Needs: an NVIDIA graphics card (for CUDA) and the option to upgrade in the future

Why I chose each part

Processor · Intel Core Ultra 7 265K

It has 20 cores (8 performance cores and 12 efficiency cores) and reaches 5.5 GHz. Having many cores helps to prepare the data while the GPU is training. It includes an NPU for small AI tasks and integrated graphics, so the screen still works when the GPU is at 100%. Intel reduced its price by $100 in 2025 [8].

Motherboard · MSI PRO Z890-P WIFI

Z890 chipset, ATX size, 4 DDR5 slots (up to 256 GB), 4 M.2 slots, PCIe 5.0 x16 for the GPU, WiFi 7 and 5 Gb networking [9]. The 4 RAM slots make it possible to upgrade to 64 GB when prices go down.

RAM · Kingston FURY Beast DDR5 32 GB (2×16 GB) 5600 MHz

For AI, 64 GB would be ideal, but today it costs more than €1,250 [6]. With 32 GB you can load medium-sized datasets and keep the IDE and Docker open. Because the motherboard has 4 slots, I can add another 32 GB later.

Graphics card · ASUS PRIME GeForce RTX 5070 Ti 16 GB

This is the most important part. It has 16 GB of GDDR7 VRAM [10] and 8,960 CUDA cores. The amount of VRAM decides how big a model you can train: with 8 or 12 GB you run out of memory very quickly. I chose NVIDIA because PyTorch and TensorFlow work with CUDA, and CUDA only works on NVIDIA cards.

Storage · Samsung 990 Pro 2 TB NVMe

It reads at 7,450 MB/s, so datasets load very quickly and the GPU doesn't have to wait. 2 TB gives enough space for datasets, Python environments and saved models.

Power supply · Corsair RM850e 850W 80+ Gold

The GPU can use about 300 W and the processor up to 250 W, so about 600 W in total. With 850 W there is extra room. Gold certification means it wastes less electricity during long training sessions, and it has a native 12V-2x6 connector for the GPU [11].

Cooling · Arctic Liquid Freezer III Pro 360

The 265K doesn't come with a cooler, and it can get very hot during training sessions of several hours. A 360 mm liquid cooler keeps it cool so it doesn't slow down. Arctic offers very good value for money.

Case · Lian Li Lancool 216

It has a mesh front panel so a lot of air can come in. It fits a GPU up to 392 mm long and supports the 360 mm radiator. Good airflow is very important when the GPU and the CPU are both working at full power.
